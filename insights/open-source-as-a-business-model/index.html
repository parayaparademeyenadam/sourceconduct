Open source fundamentals

Open source is a business model, not a giveaway.

Why companies choose to open source software, how it can support different commercial strategies, and why the right license depends on the product and the business behind it.

Open source is often misunderstood as software that a company gives away for free. That view misses the point. Companies can deliberately open source software because doing so helps them build a market, grow an ecosystem, accelerate adoption, improve a product, or create demand for other paid offerings.

The source code is made available under a license that grants users defined permissions. The business value may come from the software itself, from services around it, from complementary products, or from the adoption and influence that the project creates. Open source does not mean there is no business model. It means the business model works with the code being available under open source terms.

Why would a company open source its software?

There is no single reason. Open sourcing can support several strategies, sometimes at the same time.

1. Grow adoption and reduce friction

Making software available under an open source license can make it easier for developers and organizations to evaluate, adopt, integrate, and deploy it. Users can inspect the code and may be able to adapt it to their needs. Wider adoption can create demand for related commercial offerings.

Once an open source product becomes known and trusted among developers, discovery can increasingly happen organically. Developers recommend tools to colleagues, share them in communities, and include them in examples and projects. Search engines have long helped developers find the right library or tool, and AI assistants are increasingly becoming part of that discovery process. This can reduce the need to rely on expensive traditional marketing campaigns or to attend costly exhibitions and conferences just to get noticed. It does not mean marketing is never useful; it means a strong product and a good reputation can become powerful growth channels in their own right.

2. Build an ecosystem

A project may become more valuable as other developers contribute integrations, extensions, documentation, fixes, and tooling. A company can benefit from a broader ecosystem without having to build every component itself. The result depends on the quality of the project and the health of the community; opening the code does not automatically create either.

3. Sell services, support, or convenience

A company may make the software available openly while charging for implementation, support, maintenance, hosting, managed operations, or enterprise features. Customers may be able to run the software themselves, but prefer to pay for reliability, expertise, convenience, or a service-level commitment.

4. Sell a complementary product

The open source project may be one part of a larger commercial offering. A company might open source a library or developer tool while earning revenue from a platform, a hosted service, hardware, or a separate proprietary product that works with it.

5. Commercialize the software itself

Some companies offer the same code under an open source license and under a separate commercial license. This is often called dual licensing. It can be relevant when customers want to use the software under terms different from those offered by the open source license. Whether this model is available depends on copyright ownership, contributor agreements, dependencies, and the actual license structure.

The key question is not simply, “Should we make this code open source?” It is, “What do we want the software to achieve for the business, and what licensing strategy supports that goal?”

The license is part of the strategy

Open source licenses are not interchangeable labels. They define permissions and conditions for activities such as using, modifying, and distributing software. Some licenses are relatively permissive. Others impose additional conditions, particularly when software or modified versions are distributed. Specific obligations depend on the license and on what the company and its users actually do.

That difference can influence how a company positions a product, how it combines the project with other software, and what commercial options remain practical.

Permissive licenses

Licenses such as MIT and Apache-2.0 generally allow broad reuse, including in proprietary products, subject to their terms. They can help reduce barriers to adoption and make it easier for companies to build on the software. The trade-off is that others may be able to use the code in products that they distribute commercially without having to open source their own surrounding code, provided they comply with the applicable license conditions.

Copyleft licenses

Copyleft licenses, such as the GPL family, attach conditions to certain forms of redistribution. Depending on the license and the circumstances, distributing a covered work may require recipients to receive corresponding source code under the same license terms. This can help preserve users' freedoms when software is redistributed, but it can also affect how a company integrates and distributes the software.

Network-use provisions

Some licenses, including the AGPL, address particular situations in which users interact with modified software (which does not necessarily mean that the source code itself was changed) over a network. The exact requirements depend on the license text and the technical and deployment facts. It is risky to reduce this to a slogan such as “AGPL means you must open source your whole application.” That is not a reliable description of every situation.

Start with the product and its positioning

Choosing a license should follow a clear view of the product and the role it plays in the business. Consider questions such as:

  • What is the product? A standalone library, developer tool, application, platform, or hosted service can create different commercial and technical considerations.
  • Who should adopt it? Individual developers, large enterprises, OEMs, system integrators, and service providers may have different expectations and constraints.
  • Where will the revenue come from? The software license itself, support, hosting, enterprise capabilities, services, complementary products, or commercial licensing may each point toward a different strategy.
  • How will customers use and distribute it? Embedding, linking, modifying, redistributing, and offering software over a network are not the same activity.
  • What ecosystem do you want to encourage? The company may prioritize maximum adoption, reciprocal sharing, contributions from users, or a particular balance between community and commercial development.
  • What constraints already exist? Third-party dependencies, contributor ownership, existing license commitments, trademarks, patents, and governance can limit the options.

These questions should be considered together. Choosing a license because it is popular, or because a competitor uses it, can lead to a mismatch between the company's intentions and the permissions or obligations the license actually creates.

There is no universally best open source license

A permissive license may be a sensible choice for a company seeking broad adoption and a large ecosystem. A copyleft license may fit a strategy that emphasizes reciprocal sharing when covered software is redistributed. A network copyleft license may be relevant when the company wants to address certain forms of modified software being offered over a network. A dual-licensing model may be viable where the company controls the necessary rights and has a clear commercial reason to offer alternative terms.

These are strategic considerations, not automatic rules. The same license can work well for one company and poorly for another. Product architecture, customer expectations, distribution model, dependencies, and the company's ability to maintain the project all matter.

Open source is a business decision with technical consequences

Publishing source code is only one part of the decision. A sustainable strategy connects the product's purpose, the intended community, the commercial model, and the license. It also requires the company to understand the software it is releasing and the obligations that come with the code it incorporates.

Done deliberately, open source can be a powerful way to build adoption, trust, and commercial opportunity. Done without a clear strategy, it can create uncertainty about what the company can offer, what users may do, and how the project should evolve.

The best starting point is not “Which license should we choose?” It is “What business are we building around this software?” The license should support that answer.
